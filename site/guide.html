Part 1

What is this, really?

QuantDesk does two things, and it helps to keep them separate in your head:

  1. Backtesting — "if I had followed this rule for the last eight years, what would have happened?" It downloads real historical stock prices, applies a trading rule to them day by day, subtracts estimated trading costs, and reports the result.
  2. Paper trading — "let me follow this rule starting today, with pretend money, and see how it goes." You get an imaginary account with $100,000 of imaginary cash, and the software keeps a ledger of what you'd own and what it would be worth.

Paper trading vs. real trading

When you trade for real, you send orders to a brokerage, real dollars move, and you can lose them. Paper trading is the flight simulator version: the prices are real, the decisions are real, but the money is made up. Every fill is recorded in a small database file on your own computer. If a strategy loses 30% in paper trading, you have learned something valuable and it cost you nothing.

Paper trading is how professionals validate ideas too, because it catches things a backtest cannot: you experience the waiting, the drawdowns, the days the data source is down — in real time, in calendar order, without the ability to peek ahead.

Why this tool refuses to touch real money

This is a deliberate design decision, not a missing feature. There is no code path in QuantDesk that can place a real order:

  • The built-in broker is a local simulation — a SQLite file on your disk. It cannot talk to any exchange.
  • The one optional external integration is Alpaca's paper API (a brokerage's official pretend-money sandbox), and the adapter is written to reject any endpoint other than the paper one. You cannot point it at a real account even on purpose.

Why so strict? Because the strategies in this toolkit are decades-old published ideas. Whatever edge they once had is known to every professional on Earth and has likely been competed away, especially after costs. A tool like this is for learning how strategy evaluation works — what the metrics mean, where the traps are — not for making money. Wiring it to real money would mostly be a machine for converting overconfidence into losses.

Everything this software prints is simulated. Nothing in this guide or in any QuantDesk output is investment advice. If you take one sentence away from this page: a good backtest is a reason to be suspicious, not a reason to invest. Part 5 explains why.

Part 2

How to read a tearsheet

A tearsheet is a one-page report card for a strategy. QuantDesk writes one as a single HTML file you open in your browser (here is a real one). This section walks through every block on it, top to bottom. All the examples use a starting pot of $10,000 to keep the arithmetic concrete.

The headline metric cards

CAGR — compound annual growth rate. "How fast did the money grow per year, on average, with compounding?" If $10,000 became $20,000 after five years, the CAGR is about 14.9%, because growing 14.9% a year for five years doubles your money. It is the single most quoted number and the single most seductive one — it says nothing about how rough the ride was. A 15% CAGR that passed through a 50% crash is a very different experience from a smooth 15%.

Ann. vol — annualized volatility. "How much does the portfolio's value wobble?" Technically it is the standard deviation of daily returns, scaled to a yearly number. The intuition: with 15% annualized volatility, swings of roughly ±15% over a year are ordinary, and noticeably bigger swings happen regularly. On $10,000, 15% vol means a typical day moves your account by about $95 up or down (15% divided by the square root of 252 trading days), and bad weeks of −$400 or −$600 are completely normal. Volatility is the price you pay, in stomach acid, for returns.

Sharpe ratio. "How much return per unit of wobble?" It is roughly return divided by volatility (QuantDesk uses a risk-free rate of zero). Two strategies both earning 12%: the one that gets there with half the turbulence has twice the Sharpe. Rules of thumb for daily-data backtests like these: below 0.5 is weak, around 1.0 is good, 2.0 is suspicious, 3.0+ usually means a bug or cheating (see Part 5). Sharpe matters more than CAGR for comparing strategies, because you can always dial raw return up or down by taking more or less risk — Sharpe measures the quality of the risk you took.

Sortino ratio. Sharpe's fairer sibling. Sharpe punishes all wobble, including pleasant upside surprises; Sortino only counts downside wobble. A strategy with frequent small gains and occasional big up-days gets a Sortino noticeably above its Sharpe. Read it the same way, just with slightly higher thresholds.

Max drawdown. The single most important number for a human being. It answers: "from the worst possible peak, how far down did the account go before recovering?" A 30% max drawdown means that at some point, $10,000 of your money became $7,000. The dates on the card mark the decline itself — from the peak to the bottom of the valley; how long the climb back took is visible on the drawdown chart below, where the full width of a spike is the time spent underwater. Before you admire any CAGR, ask: would I genuinely have kept following the rules while down $3,000, with no guarantee it ever comes back? Most people would not — they sell at the bottom, which converts a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss. Note the convention: QuantDesk prints drawdown as a positive number ("29.27%" means a 29.27% loss).

Calmar ratio. CAGR divided by max drawdown — "how much annual growth per unit of worst-case pain?" A Calmar of 0.5 means the worst valley was twice as deep as one year's typical growth. Higher is better; above 1 is rare over long periods.

Hit rate. The percentage of trading days the strategy made money, out of days it was actually invested. Surprisingly close to a coin flip for almost everything — the good strategies here sit around 51–57%. That is normal and fine: profits come from winning slightly more often, or winning bigger than losing, compounded over thousands of days. A claimed hit rate of 80% should make you reach for your wallet — to protect it.

Equity curve vs. benchmark

The first chart shows the growth of $1: the blue line is the strategy, the gray line is the benchmark — SPY, a fund that simply tracks the S&P 500, the "do nothing clever" alternative. Two honest questions to ask of it:

  • Did it beat just buying SPY? If not, the strategy added complexity, costs, and effort for nothing.
  • Where did the money actually come from? If the whole gap over the benchmark comes from one lucky stretch (one crash dodged, one mania ridden), the strategy is one historical accident, not a repeatable process.

Drawdown chart

This is the equity curve's shadow: it plots, for every day, how far below its own previous peak the portfolio sat. It hugs zero in good times and digs spikes downward in bad ones. Look at the width of the spikes, not just the depth — a 20% loss that takes two years to recover is psychologically much harder than a 30% loss that snaps back in three months. This chart is where you rehearse the question from the max-drawdown card: could I sit through that?

Rolling 60-day Sharpe

The headline Sharpe is one number averaged over eight years; this chart shows the Sharpe recomputed over a sliding ~3-month window, so you can see when the strategy was working. Every real strategy spends long stretches below zero — months where it was losing money. That is what following a strategy actually feels like from the inside, and it is the chart that best explains why people abandon good strategies at the worst moment.

Monthly returns table

A grid of each calendar month's return. Scan it for character rather than precision: How bad is a bad month? Do losses cluster (three, four red months in a row)? Did it survive the months you remember being scary? A strategy that earns its CAGR in two spectacular months and treads water otherwise is a different animal from one that grinds steadily — same CAGR, very different to live with.

Turnover and costs

The most underrated block on the page. Turnover is how much of the portfolio gets bought or sold; "annualized turnover 5.6×" means that over a year, the strategy trades about 5.6 times the value of the whole account. Every trade pays costs — QuantDesk assumes 3 basis points (0.03%, i.e. $3 per $10,000 traded) for commission plus slippage — and the "total cost deducted" row shows what that subtracted over the whole backtest. The crucial check: compare costs to the strategy's edge over the benchmark. A high-turnover strategy whose advantage is smaller than double its assumed costs has no real-world advantage at all, because real costs are usually worse than assumed (see Part 5).

Every number above comes from one specific simulated history with assumed costs. The footer on each tearsheet says it plainly: simulated performance is hypothetical, routinely overstates live results, and is not investment advice.

Part 3

Your first 10 minutes

You will need: a Mac or Linux machine (Windows works too), Python 3.10 or newer, and a terminal. The terminal is the text window where you type commands: on a Mac, press ⌘-Space, type "Terminal", press Enter. Lines starting with $ below are what you type (don't type the $); everything else is what the computer prints back.

Step 1 — download and install

First, check what Python you have — QuantDesk needs Python 3.10 or newer, and the version that ships with a Mac is older than that:

$ python3 --version
Python 3.12.4

If that prints 3.9-point-anything or lower (or "command not found"), install a current Python first: download the installer from python.org/downloads, run it like any other app, then close and reopen the terminal and check the version again.

$ git clone https://github.com/fzheng96/quantdesk
$ cd quantdesk
$ python3 -m venv .venv
$ source .venv/bin/activate
$ pip install -e .

Line by line: the first command copies the project to your computer; the second moves into its folder; the third creates a virtual environment — a private folder of Python packages just for this project, so nothing touches the rest of your system; the fourth switches your terminal into it (the prompt grows a (.venv) prefix); the fifth installs QuantDesk. After this, the quantdesk command works whenever the virtual environment is active. Each time you come back in a new terminal window, run cd quantdesk and source .venv/bin/activate again before using it.

Three things that may come up. On a brand-new Mac, the first git command pops up a dialog offering to install the "command line developer tools" — click Install and rerun the command once it finishes; that is expected. If pip install refuses with an externally-managed-environment error, it means the virtual environment is not active — run the python3 -m venv .venv and source .venv/bin/activate lines and try again. And if pip is not found, use pip3.

Step 2 — run the demo

$ quantdesk demo

This downloads daily prices for 20 large US stocks plus the SPY benchmark from free sources, runs all four strategies, prints a comparison table, and writes a tearsheet to reports/demo.html. The first run takes a while because it is downloading years of prices; it saves them in a local cache, so later runs are fast. Open reports/demo.html in your browser and walk through it with Part 2 of this guide open beside it.

If the free data sources are unreachable, the demo finishes on clearly labeled SYNTHETIC DATA — computer-generated random prices. Useful to check the install works; meaningless as research. Just rerun later.

Step 3 — compare the strategies yourself

 meanrev    17.01%     24.94%     0.75      1.12   35.74%     0.48     50.89%
 tsmom      16.52%     15.36%     1.07      1.53   29.27%     0.56     56.79%
 xsmom      28.43%     25.76%     1.10      1.59   37.39%     0.76     55.99%

Figures are net of assumed commission and slippage. Simulated results; not
investment advice.

(Real output from a real run; your end date and numbers will differ slightly as new days of data arrive.) Now practice reading it:

  • xsmom has the biggest CAGR — and the deepest drawdown (37%) and the highest volatility. The return came from taking more risk in a period when risk was paid.
  • tsmom and dma earned less but with far gentler rides — look at the Sharpe column, where tsmom (1.07) nearly matches xsmom (1.10).
  • meanrev has the worst Sharpe and a hit rate of almost exactly a coin flip: it took xsmom-sized risk for tsmom-sized returns. On this data, in this period, it was the weakest of the four.

Step 4 — run one strategy with a tearsheet

$ quantdesk backtest --strategy tsmom --start 2018-01-01 --report reports/tsmom.html

Then open reports/tsmom.html. Try variations: --strategy dma, a different --start, your own --tickers AAPL,MSFT,KO, or --vol-target (which scales positions down in turbulent times, targeting a steadier 10%-volatility ride). Watching how much the numbers move when you nudge the inputs is itself a lesson — see Part 5.

Part 4

The paper trading loop

Backtests look backward; paper trading goes forward. The loop has three commands — scan (what does the strategy want?), paper-apply (do it with pretend money), paper-status (how is it going?) — which you repeat every day or week, like watering a plant. Your pretend account starts with $100,000 in cash the first time you touch it.

Reading the first table: the dual-moving-average strategy looked at all 20 stocks in the universe and found 10 currently in an uptrend (50-day average above 200-day average). Reading the second table, column by column:

ColumnMeaning
SymbolThe stock's ticker.
Sidebuy or sell — which direction the trade goes.
QtyNumber of shares. Fractional, because the simulator sizes positions exactly — 16.913 shares of AAPL is precisely $5,000 worth.
Last closeThe most recent end-of-day price the order is based on.

scan only prints these suggestions. Nothing has happened yet — no money, real or pretend, has moved.

Two details are doing quiet teaching here:

  • Fill price vs. last close. AAPL closed at 295.63 but you "paid" 295.69. The simulator applies slippage — 2 basis points (0.02%) — against you on every fill, because in real life you never quite get the printed price. The Cost column is that haircut in dollars: $5,000 × 0.02% = $1.00 per order.
  • Equity dropped instantly. You started at $100,000; one rebalance later you hold $49,990 cash plus $50,000 of stock = $99,990. Ten dollars evaporated into trading friction before the market moved at all. Multiply by a year of rebalances and you understand why Part 5 is obsessed with costs.

Cash is uninvested money; Value is each position marked at the latest cached closing price; Equity = cash + sum of values — the number to watch over weeks. Below these, the command also lists your recent fills, a paper trail of every trade the simulator executed.

The loop, ongoing

Days later, prices have moved and maybe some stocks crossed their moving averages. Run scan again: now it suggests only the differences — sell what fell out of the signal, buy what entered, trim and top up the rest. paper-apply executes them; paper-status shows the running result. If the portfolio already matches the targets, QuantDesk says so and trades nothing.

One practical gotcha: the ledger lives in data/paper.sqlite relative to the folder you run the command from. Run from a different folder and you get a fresh, empty $100,000 account — your "missing" portfolio is still in the old folder. Always run from the same directory (the printed Ledger: path tells you which file it used), or pass --db with a full path.

A worthwhile experiment: paper trade a strategy for three months, then backtest the same strategy over those same three months. The gap between the two results is your personal, measured introduction to Part 5.

Part 5

How to not fool yourself

The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and in backtesting, you are the easiest person to fool. Great-looking backtests are easy to produce by accident. Here are the standard ways, and what QuantDesk does (and cannot do) about each.

1. Ignoring costs

Every trade pays a commission, and pays slippage — the gap between the price on your screen and the price you actually get. Three basis points sounds like nothing: $3 per $10,000 traded. But a strategy that turns over its whole portfolio 20 times a year pays 20 × 0.03% = 0.6% of the account annually — and that is QuantDesk's optimistic assumption, calibrated to huge liquid stocks. Trade anything smaller and real slippage can be several times worse. Many published "anomalies" earn 1–2% a year on paper and die completely once realistic costs are charged. QuantDesk always nets out costs and shows the deduction on the tearsheet; your job is to remember the assumed number is a floor, not an estimate.

2. Lookahead bias — trading on tomorrow's newspaper

The most dangerous bug in backtesting: accidentally letting the simulation use information that did not exist yet. The classic version is using today's closing price to decide to buy at today's close — in reality, by the time you know the close, the market is shut. Results from a leaky backtest look fantastic and mean nothing.

QuantDesk's engine enforces a one-day lag by construction: a decision made using day t's prices earns day t+1's return, with no exceptions. The test suite includes a "canary": a deliberately cheating strategy that bets on tomorrow's known return. Fed through the engine, the canary must come out with roughly zero performance — if it ever shows a profit, the engine has sprung a leak and the test fails. You should demand this kind of self-test from any backtesting tool you trust.

3. Overfitting — torturing the data until it confesses

Try 50 variations of a strategy and pick the one with the best historical numbers, and you have mostly selected for luck. The past has a fixed set of wiggles; with enough knobs you can fit them perfectly and predict nothing. Warning signs: performance collapses when a parameter changes slightly (a 50/200-day rule that is great while 45/190 is mediocre has memorized history, not discovered a truth); the pitch needs many qualifiers ("only on Tuesdays, except December"); the backtest improves every time you add a rule. The strategies in QuantDesk deliberately have only two or three parameters each, set to the boring values from decades-old literature — fewer knobs, less room to fool yourself. The defaults are still parameter choices made with hindsight, which is why the docs call a good backtest at one setting "weak evidence by itself."

4. Walk-forward — the honest version of testing

The cure for overfitting is to never grade a strategy on data it was tuned on. Walk-forward analysis does this mechanically: tune on 2015–2018, test on 2019; slide forward, tune on 2016–2019, test on 2020; and so on. Stitch together only the out-of-sample test segments, and you get a fairer picture of how the strategy performs on data it has never seen. The out-of-sample result is almost always worse than the tuned backtest — the size of that gap is a direct measurement of how much fooling-yourself was going on. QuantDesk's engine ships walk-forward analysis as a library function for exactly this purpose.

5. Survivorship bias

QuantDesk's default universe is 20 of today's biggest US companies — companies we know, today, turned out to be winners. A backtest over them is quietly asking "how would these strategies have done on stocks that we already know succeeded?" Enron, Lehman Brothers, and a hundred forgotten near-misses are not on the list. This inflates every number on the site, and it is the single biggest caveat on the comparison table. There is no fix within free data; there is only remembering it every time a CAGR impresses you.

6. Why great backtests usually disappoint live

Put it together. A backtest is the strategy's best possible self: it is selected (you only ever see ideas that backtested well — the failures were deleted), it is fitted to one specific past that will not repeat, it trades at optimistic costs with perfect discipline, on a universe of known survivors. Live trading strips away every one of those advantages, and adds a new one: the strategy is public, and every edge that is public gets competed away. The practical rule professionals use: take the backtest Sharpe and cut it in half; assume the live drawdown will be deeper than the worst one in the backtest. If the idea still looks worth following — in paper trading — at half its Sharpe and 1.5× its drawdown, it might be interesting. If it only looks good at full backtest glory, it was never good.

None of this means backtesting is useless. It means a backtest is a filter for discarding bad ideas, not a forecast of profits. QuantDesk exists to let you run that filter honestly — and to let you feel, in paper trading, what the surviving ideas are actually like to live with.

Part 6

Glossary

Backtest
A simulation of how a trading rule would have performed on historical prices. Always an overestimate of live performance; see Part 5.
Basis point (bp)
One hundredth of a percent. 1 bp = 0.01%; 100 bps = 1%. On $10,000, one basis point is $1. Costs are quoted in basis points because they are tiny per trade and deadly in aggregate.
Benchmark
The simple alternative you must beat to justify your complexity. Here: SPY, a fund tracking the S&P 500 index of large US stocks.
CAGR (compound annual growth rate)
The steady yearly growth rate that would turn the starting value into the ending value over the same period. $10,000 → $20,000 in 5 years ≈ 14.9% CAGR.
Calmar ratio
CAGR divided by maximum drawdown. Growth per unit of worst-case pain.
Close / closing price
The last traded price of the day. QuantDesk works entirely with daily closes.
Drawdown
How far the portfolio currently sits below its own highest point so far. Maximum drawdown is the deepest such valley over the whole period — printed as a positive number ("30%" = a 30% peak-to-trough loss).
Equity / equity curve
The total value of the account (cash + positions) over time. The equity curve is its chart, usually normalized to a starting value of $1.
Fill
An executed order: the moment "buy 16.91 shares" becomes an actual position, at a specific price.
Hit rate
The share of invested days the strategy made money. Good strategies are often barely above 50%.
Lookahead bias
A simulation accidentally using information not yet available at decision time. Produces spectacular, meaningless backtests. QuantDesk guards against it with a forced one-day lag and a tripwire test.
Long / long-only
Owning something, profiting if it rises. A long-only strategy never bets on declines; its only defensive move is holding cash. All four QuantDesk strategies are long-only.
Mean reversion
The tendency of short-term price overshoots to bounce back; also the family of strategies that bet on the bounce.
Momentum
The tendency of recent winners to keep winning over months. Time-series momentum compares an asset to its own past; cross-sectional momentum compares it to its peers.
Moving average
The average price over the last N days, recomputed daily — a smoothed version of the price used to read trends through the noise.
Paper trading
Trading with simulated money at real prices. All QuantDesk trading is paper trading.
Portfolio weight
The fraction of the account in one asset. "AAPL at 5%" = $5,000 of a $100,000 account in Apple. Unallocated weight sits in cash.
Rebalance
Trading the portfolio back to its target weights after prices and signals drift.
Sharpe ratio
Return per unit of volatility. The standard quality score for strategies: ~1 is good for daily-data backtests, 3+ usually means an error.
Slippage
The gap between the price you expected and the price you actually got, always against you on average. QuantDesk assumes 2 bps per trade.
Sortino ratio
Like Sharpe, but only penalizing downside volatility.
SPY
An exchange-traded fund tracking the S&P 500. QuantDesk's benchmark.
Survivorship bias
Testing on assets known today to have survived and thrived, which inflates results. The default universe here has it; remember that.
Tearsheet
A one-page performance report for a strategy. Part 2 walks through every block.
Ticker
A stock's short exchange symbol: AAPL is Apple, KO is Coca-Cola, SPY is the S&P 500 fund.
Turnover
How much of the portfolio is traded. Annualized turnover of 5× means a year's trades total five times the account value. Turnover × cost-per-trade = the strategy's friction bill.
Universe
The set of assets a strategy is allowed to choose from. Default here: 20 large US stocks.
Volatility (vol)
How much returns wobble, annualized. 15% vol ≈ ±15% swings in an ordinary year, with worse entirely possible.
Volatility targeting
Automatically shrinking positions when markets get turbulent and re-expanding when they calm, aiming for a steadier ride (QuantDesk's --vol-target aims at 10% annualized).
Walk-forward analysis
Repeatedly tuning a strategy on one stretch of history and grading it only on the following, unseen stretch. The honest way to test; see Part 5.
Z-score
How unusual a value is versus its own recent history, in units of standard deviations. The mean-reversion strategy buys when the 5-day return's z-score is below −1.5: "this drop is large compared to this stock's recent behavior."

All performance figures in this guide are simulated research results produced from free historical price data, net of assumed commission and slippage. Simulated performance is hypothetical: it reflects no actual positions, ignores taxes, and routinely overstates what live trading would have achieved. Past performance does not predict future results. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell any security. Do not trade real money based on output from this software.
