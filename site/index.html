QuantDesk

A small, honest toolkit for studying trading strategies with historical data and pretend money — and for learning why most trading ideas are worse than they look.

What this is

QuantDesk is a free command-line program that tests simple, decades-old trading strategies against real historical stock prices, subtracting assumed (deliberately simple) trading costs, so you can see how they would have performed. It also includes a paper-trading mode: a pretend brokerage account that starts with $100,000 of imaginary money, so you can follow a strategy day by day without risking a cent. There is deliberately no way to connect it to real money — it is a learning and research tool, not a trading product.

Never used a terminal or traded a stock? Start with the beginner's guide — it assumes nothing.

What each strategy believes

tsmomTime-series momentum

The belief: stocks that have been rising tend to keep rising for a while, because news spreads gradually and investors react slowly, then pile in. This strategy holds every stock whose own trailing one-year return (skipping the most recent month) is positive, in equal slices. It has historically worked across many markets and decades, which is why it is the default here. It fails badly at sharp V-shaped turning points — it is still holding after a peak and still in cash after a bottom — and it bleeds money in choppy, sideways markets where the signal flips back and forth and every flip pays trading costs.

xsmomCross-sectional momentum

The belief: it is not whether a stock went up that matters, but whether it went up more than its peers — relative winners over the past 3–12 months tend to stay winners. This strategy ranks the universe by trailing return and holds only the top five names. Because it always holds the five best-ranked stocks no matter what, it stays fully invested through bear markets, holding the "least bad" losers, and it gets hit hardest right after a crash, when the previous losers rebound fastest and the portfolio owns exactly the wrong things. Holding only five names also means one bad company can do real damage, and frequent rank changes mean high turnover and high costs.

meanrevMean reversion

The belief: over a few days, prices overshoot — a sudden sharp drop is often panic or forced selling rather than real news, and the price tends to bounce back. This strategy buys stocks whose five-day return is unusually negative compared to their own recent history, and sells once the move normalizes. Its failure mode is the famous "falling knife": sometimes a crash is real news (an earnings collapse, a fraud) and there is no bounce, only further losses — and the strategy has no way to tell the difference. It also loses persistently in strongly trending markets, and when only one stock is signaling, the entire portfolio concentrates into precisely the most distressed name.

dmaDual moving average

The belief: you do not need to predict anything — just stay invested while the trend is up and step aside when it turns down. This strategy holds a stock while its 50-day average price is above its 200-day average price. Its real value historically has been avoiding the deepest, longest crashes, which matter more to long-run compounding than any single good day. The price of that protection: it is always late (by the time the averages cross, part of the move is already over, in both directions), and in range-bound markets the averages cross back and forth repeatedly, paying trading costs each time for no benefit.
